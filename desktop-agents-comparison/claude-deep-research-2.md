## The short version

The R5 Mark II keeps the same 45-megapixel resolution and broadly the same image quality as the R5, but it's a substantial performance upgrade everywhere else: a new backside-illuminated sensor, much faster readout, faster burst rates, a deeper buffer, a smarter autofocus system, Eye Control AF, and stronger video specs. The reviewer calls the Mark II "the best general-purpose model ever designed" and notes that he replaced his R5 bodies with three R5 Mark II cameras.

## Sensor and image quality

Both cameras use a 45 MP full-frame sensor with identical pixel dimensions (8192 x 5464) and the same DLA of f/7.1. The R5 uses a conventional CMOS sensor; the R5 Mark II's backside-illuminated (BSI) sensor is new, bringing dramatically improved performance, mostly in speed rather than resolution.

In terms of pure image quality, the two are very close. R5 II image noise levels match those of the R5 — both are excellent performers. On dynamic range, the R5 Mark II shows a tiny disadvantage, primarily in the cyan block, to the R5, but the reviewer notes you won't likely notice this difference in normal use. So if image quality alone is your deciding factor, there's little separating them.

The Mark II does add in-camera Neural Network upscaling (doubling dimensions to ~179 MP) and improved noise-reduction processing that previously required a subscription.

The Mark II captures up to 30 fps full-bit depth (14) lossless compressed RAW with full AF and AE and electronic shutter, versus 20 fps for the R5. The readout-speed jump is dramatic — the R5's imaging sensor readout speed is fast, but the R5 II is considerably faster and nearly as fast as the R3, despite its dramatically higher pixel count — which means much less rolling-shutter distortion when using the silent electronic shutter.

The standout new feature is pre-continuous shooting. The pre-continuous shooting feature collects up to 15 frames from immediately before the full shutter release press and continues to shoot until you release or the buffer fills. Unlike Canon's lower models, the R5 II's pre-continuous shooting mode stores the individual image files, including RAW format if selected, to the card, eliminating the after-capture delay.

## Autofocus

Both have excellent autofocus, but the Mark II is meaningfully smarter thanks to its new DIGIC X plus DIGIC Accelerator processor pairing. It adds Action Priority AF (recognizing specific plays in soccer, basketball, volleyball, and American football), subject-type Auto detection, registered-face prioritization, and an improved Eye Control AF that lets you place the focus point just by looking at the subject.

Low-light AF also improves: the R5 focuses down to EV -6, while the R5 II can AF at EV -7.5, making it Canon's best-rated low-light focusing camera, equal to the R1 and R3.

## Stabilization and video

IBIS goes from up to 8 stops on the R5 to up to 8.5 stops of shake correction on the Mark II, which also adds Peripheral Coordinated Control to reduce edge "wobble" with compatible lenses.

For video, the R5 tops out at 8K 30p; the Mark II reaches up to 8K DCI (full width) 60p RAW, 4K DCI 120p MP4, 2K DCI 240p, adds Canon Log 2, internal/HDMI RAW output, and notably better overheating behavior — with the optional cooling fan grip, 4K 60p runs essentially unlimited.

## Price and verdict

The R5 has been discontinued and replaced by the Mark II, so the R5 is now mainly a used-market option, while the Mark II carries a higher (the review calls it "moderately expensive") price. The reviewer's quirks with the Mark II are minor: an annoying IBIS hum in quiet rooms, and some nagging on-screen messages.

Who should pick which: if you shoot sports, wildlife, fast action, or video, the Mark II's speed, buffer, pre-capture, and AF upgrades are well worth it. If you mainly shoot stills of slower subjects — portraits, landscapes, general use — the R5 delivers nearly identical image quality and can be a strong value on the used market.
